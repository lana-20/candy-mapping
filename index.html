What sixty automated runs found in a site built to break them

CandyMapper calls itself “The Website That Goes Wrong!” and dares you to find the issues. Here is the inventory automation came back with — including one defect no manual tester can reach, and that your fastest users hit.

LB Lana Begunova Jul 27, 2026 · 13 min read

Paul Grossman — the Dark Arts Wizard — built a sandbox for testers to break their tools against. His pop-up challenge on CandyMapper looks like a warm-up exercise. Close a modal. Fill in the contact form. Verify a success message. The page itself taunts you underneath: “Found a misspelled word? There are 11 more issues and 8 Horror Movie references. Happy hunting!”

I went hunting with automation instead of a mouse: one eight-step end-to-end test, sixty runs across three sessions. I drove it two ways, both with Vibium — once as a script the command line executes top to bottom, and once through its MCP server, with an AI agent deciding each step as it went. Every run passed all eight steps. Along the way it turned up three genuine defects, two behaviors that will quietly wreck a test suite, and one false alarm that taught me more than the real bugs did.

Defect one: the first click into the void

Confirmed · 30 of 30 runs

The first Submit click after page load is silently discarded

Vulnerable for ~4s after load · impact: a fast customer loses their enquiry with no error shown

Submit the contact form and nothing happens. No validation error, no network request, no visible reaction at all. Click the same button a second time and everything works perfectly.

My first assumption was that my own tooling had missed the target. So I attached a capture-phase listener to the button, counted the clicks the page actually received, and watched the network at the same time: 

after click 1 → listener=1  xhr=[]  message=false
after click 2 → listener=2  xhr=["POST .../v3/messages"]

The page receives the first click — the counter increments — and then does nothing with it. A plain element.click() typed into the console behaves identically, so this is not an automation artefact. It is a startup race inside the page: the button is mounted and clickable before the handler that submits the form has finished wiring up.

For a real customer the failure mode is the worst kind. They fill in the form, click Submit, see nothing happen, and either click again or leave. There is no error message, because as far as the page is concerned nothing went wrong. Whatever fraction of your visitors click faster than your page hydrates, that fraction silently never reaches you.

Defect two: a reply within 48 years

Confirmed · 30 of 30 runs

“Thank you for your inquiry! We will get back to you within 48 Years.”

Impact: customer-facing copy on the successful submission path

Not a typo — a units bug, and my favorite kind, because it is the sort that survives review precisely because it reads as fluent English. Every automated run matched the string exactly. The challenge text on the pop-up has its own companion, asking you to “Verify any success massage,” which I choose to believe is deliberate.

Defect three: the form rebuilds itself underneath you

Confirmed · all 3 sessions

Every form field is assigned a new id on each re-render

Impact: breaks id-based automation, and any integration keyed on field identity

This is the one that cost me the most time, and it is the most instructive. I read the field ids, confirmed the first one was visible, and then failed to type into it — because between those two instructions the page had replaced every input: 

# the same four fields, seconds apart
ids        → ["input5878","input5879","input5880","input5881"]
is visible → true
fill       → element not found
ids        → ["input11","input12","input13","input14"]   ← renamed mid-instruction

The ids are sequential counters handed out at render time, so they change whenever the component rebuilds — which on this page is often, and unpredictably. Anything that holds a reference to a field is holding a receipt for a field that no longer exists.

The workaround is to key on something the page treats as identity rather than as a label. This form ships data-aid attributes that survive the rebuild, and switching to them took the suite from intermittently green to a hundred per cent: 

fill 'input[data-aid="First Name"]'          "Lana"
fill 'input[data-aid="CONTACT_FORM_EMAIL"]' "lana@daisyladybug.com"
click 'button[data-aid="CONTACT_SUBMIT_BUTTON_REND"]'

Worth saying plainly: regenerating ids is not only a testing problem. Anything downstream that identifies a field by id — analytics, session replay, autofill heuristics, assistive technology bindings — is looking at a moving target.

Two behaviors that are not bugs, but will cost you a day

The pop-up only appears on a cold cache. It fires once per session. If your test does not clear cookies first, step two sits there waiting for a modal that is never coming, and you will spend an hour convincing yourself your selector is wrong. It isn't.

Only two fields are genuinely required. The form presents first name, last name, email, phone and message, with an SMS opt-in notice attached to the phone field that implies it matters. It does not. The form submits successfully with phone and message empty; only first name and email are enforced. If you are writing test data, or reasoning about what the business actually captures, that gap between the presented form and the enforced form is worth knowing.

The false alarm, and what it taught me

I nearly solidified a fourth defect: that the success message flashed and vanished too quickly to assert against, and that you needed a MutationObserver armed in advance to catch it.

That was wrong. The message sits in the DOM, visible, for at least thirty seconds. A one-line wait-for-text assertion catches it every time, three times out of three.

The reason I got it wrong is the interesting part. Every check that came back empty had run after a swallowed first click — so nothing had been submitted and there was no message to find. Defect one was impersonating a rendering bug. And the single check I ran after a genuine submission used a pattern that could not match the real wording, so it obligingly confirmed my mistake.

Two independent errors that happened to agree. That is the shape of most wrong conclusions I have reached — never one big mistake, but two small ones pointing the same way.

I only caught it by re-running everything from scratch and checking each published claim against a fresh measurement. Ten findings survived that audit unchanged. One did not, and it was the one I had felt most clever about.

Why a human tester will never find defect one

Here is the part I did not expect. I ran the same eight steps two ways — a scripted runner executing a fixed sequence, and an AI agent deciding each step as it went. Both passed every step of every run. But only one of them ever saw the click bug.

Because it is a startup race, arrival time is the only thing that matters. The scripted run reaches Submit about a second after filling the fields and is always too early. The agent takes around twenty-five seconds to get there, by which point the page is fully wired and behaves impeccably. A human moving a mouse and reading labels is firmly in the second category.

On that zero build time. Every other entry on the grid reports how long it took to author and debug a test. The agent-driven entry has no test to author — I described the eight steps in a sentence and the agent drove the browser live, deciding each action as it went. There is no script, so there is nothing to build and nothing to maintain.

That is not a free lunch, and plotting it at zero slightly flatters it. The authoring cost has not vanished; it has moved into every single run, which is exactly what the 49.5 seconds is. Run the suite once and the agent wins outright. Run it a thousand times in CI and the scripted entry has paid its 8.6 minutes back many times over — and it is the only one of the two whose timing is steady enough to alert on.

I want to be careful here, because the tempting move is to look at that spread and imply that the slower half of the field misses the bug. I have not tested any of those tools, so I have no idea which of them catch it. What I did instead was measure the thing that decides it.

The window is four seconds wide

If arrival time is the only variable, then the defect has a measurable expiry. So I ran the same journey repeatedly, varying only how long I waited before clicking Submit, and recorded whether the submission landed: 

click at t+2788ms  after load → SWALLOWED
click at t+2866ms  after load → SWALLOWED
click at t+2869ms  after load → SWALLOWED
click at t+3687ms  after load → SWALLOWED
click at t+3688ms  after load → SWALLOWED
click at t+4199ms  after load → worked
click at t+4268ms  after load → worked
click at t+7200ms  after load → worked
click at t+11858ms after load → worked
click at t+22364ms after load → worked

The boundary sits between 3.7 and 4.2 seconds after page load, and it is sharp — 3,687ms is discarded, 4,199ms goes through, and it reproduces on both sides. Click Submit inside that window and the page eats it. Click after, and everything works exactly as designed.

That turns a vague worry into a two-minute self-check that anyone can run in any framework, against their own tooling:

  1. Load the page with a cold cache and dismiss the modal.
  2. Fill first name and email — those are the only two required fields.
  3. Click Submit less than four seconds after page load.
  4. Watch the network. No request means the click was discarded. Click again and it fires.

So I am not claiming any of those tools miss it. I am saying the question has a precise answer for each of them, the experiment costs two minutes, and the grid tells you nothing about the outcome by itself. If you run it against your own suite, I would genuinely like to know what you find.

The four-second figure does one more thing: it tells you who is affected in production. Not the visitor typing carefully into five fields — they were never going to make it. It is the visitor whose browser autofills the form for them, who clicks Submit almost immediately, and who gets no error and no confirmation. The fastest, most efficient users are the ones this defect selects for.

So this defect is invisible to manual testing, invisible to slow automation, and reproduces a hundred per cent of the time to anything quick. It is not flaky. It is perfectly deterministic — you simply have to be fast enough to be there when the page is still vulnerable.

That reframes test speed as something other than a CI cost line. A suite is an instrument, and how fast it moves determines which class of defect it can perceive at all. If everything in your pipeline is slow, an entire category of hydration and startup races is simply outside your field of view.

The hunt so far

Paul says there are eleven more issues and eight horror movie references waiting on that page. Automation found three defects and two trip-hazards in the contact-form journey alone, which suggests the rest of the site is generously stocked.

If you want to go hunting yourself, the three things that actually moved the needle for me were: assert on the negative path rather than only the happy one, because the validation error was the first thing to reveal the page's real behavior; never key locators on generated ids; and run everything at least three times before you believe your own findings.

The fourth would be: be quick about it. That is where the good bugs are hiding.

Method: an eight-step end-to-end test of the contact-form journey on candymapper.com — navigate, dismiss the modal, scroll, fill the name fields, submit without the required email, assert the validation error, fill the email, assert the confirmation. Ten runs per driving mode in each of three independent sessions on July 27 2026, sixty runs total, 480 of 480 steps passing. Both modes were Vibium v26.5.31 — the command-line runner for the scripted path, the MCP server for the agent-driven one, each on its own Chrome build. Every defect above was reproduced in all three sessions.

Update, August 4 2026 — hardened to 100 runs. Reran the same eight-step journey independently: 50 scripted-CLI runs and 50 agent-driven-MCP runs, a separate day, the fuller canonical sequence (including an explicit assertion on the validation-error step, which the original run never checked programmatically). The finding held — 49 of 50 CLI runs hit the bug, 50 of 50 MCP runs avoided it — but the margin moved. CLI's median arrival rose to about 4.7 seconds (from about 1 originally), landing much closer to the boundary; MCP's stayed close to 21 seconds. The 25× arrival gap first published above is closer to 4.6× comparing hardened medians.

Why MCP is slower is not the browser layer — both arms pay the same roughly 170–250ms per actual browser action underneath. The CLI script issues all 8 steps back to back with no thinking between them. The MCP agent re-reasons at every one of those 8 steps — reads the previous tool result, decides the next tool call, then calls it — so the gap is 8 stacked reasoning turns, not one slow step. vibium-mcp-flow.html traces one such turn stage by stage.

The boundary itself moved too — confirmed properly, by re-bisecting twice more in the same session, not by trusting a single click. First bisection: 3,687→4,199ms. Re-bisected about 45 minutes later: 3,941→4,252ms. Re-bisected again about 90 minutes after that: 4,095→4,393ms. A consistent 150–250ms creep each time — the window drifts within a single session, not only session to session, modestly and repeatably. Treat any published bracket as a snapshot of the moment it was measured, not a constant to check new data against later.

A second race turned up along the way: the validation-error click (submitting with no email) is itself subject to the identical startup race. The CLI's own attempt at it is fast enough to be swallowed too — confirmed false 0 of 50 times. The MCP agent's reasoning time carries it past the window by then — confirmed true 50 of 50 times. Even a test's own precondition check can be the thing the bug hides from.
